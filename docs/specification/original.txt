GRANADEROS: Jagged Alliance 2 Engine Historical Conversion Specification

1. Historical Context and Narrative Mapping (1810–1820)

Spanish Royalist Command Hierarchy

The structural antagonist architecture of the Jagged Alliance 2 engine—originally centered on Queen Deidranna’s centralized dictatorship in Arulco—is mapped directly to the Spanish Royalist command hierarchy operating under the authority of King Fernando VII and the Viceroyalty structure. Rather than confronting an isolated monarch in a single fortified palace, the liberation forces must dismantle a coordinated, multi-tiered colonial military apparatus operating across Argentine territory.

The Royalist opposition is organized into three operational commands that mirror the strategic threats historically faced by the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata. In the northern theater, the Royal Army of Peru represents the primary conventional threat, executing recurring counter-invasions southward through the mountain corridors of the Humahuaca gorge. General Joaquín de la Pezuela serves as the supreme Royalist field antagonist, commanding veteran Peninsular battalions and formidable artillery trains with methodical tactical discipline. Assisting him is Brigadier General Pío Tristán, who leads the aggressive forward divisions invading Jujuy and Salta, presenting an immediate operational obstacle that players must neutralize during the campaign's middle chapters.

Along the maritime and riverine frontier, Viceroy Francisco Javier de Elío and Governor Gaspar de Vigodet direct naval forces out of the fortified stronghold of Montevideo. Their riverine squadron, commanded by naval officers such as Jacinto de Romarate, conducts sustained corsair incursions and amphibious raids along the Paraná and Uruguay rivers. This naval pressure constricts maritime commerce, raids coastal settlements such as San Nicolás and San Pedro, and threatens the capital's supply lines. Meanwhile, in the interior provinces, Royalist Cabildos and Loyalist partisans organize covert counter-revolutionary networks, orchestrating sabotage against Patriot ammunition trains and raiding frontier estancias.

General José de San Martín: The Revolutionary Catalyst

In the standard campaign structure, the rebel leader Miguel Cordona is discovered early as an underground commander who joins the squad once basic trust parameters are satisfied. In GRANADEROS, General José de San Martín assumes this catalyst role through an expanded narrative arc that reflects his historical transformation from an arriving continental officer into the supreme architect of liberation. Rather than being recruited in the early game, San Martín operates as an off-map strategic mentor, administrative catalyst, and mission coordinator whose personal field deployment marks the culmination of an extensive preparation phase.

The progression begins in Buenos Aires following San Martín's arrival aboard the frigate George Canning in 1812. During this opening phase, he oversees the creation of the Cuartel de Retiro and establishes the Regimiento de Granaderos a Caballo. Players support his efforts by investing funds, acquiring quality mounts, and funneling high-strength recruits into rigorous cavalry training. The second milestone triggers when Royalist naval detachments ascend the Paraná River to forage and plunder coastal settlements. The player participates alongside San Martín in the historical encounter at San Lorenzo, executing a coordinated tactical strike that secures riverine shipping and establishes the operational prestige of the Granaderos doctrine.

Following catastrophic Patriot defeats at Vilcapugio and Ayohuma, San Martín is dispatched north to relieve General Manuel Belgrano at the Posta de Yatasto. Here, San Martín operates as a non-player strategic commander who analyzes the theater and concludes that a direct offensive through the mountainous terrain of Upper Peru is militarily non-viable. He entrusts the northern frontier entirely to Martín Miguel de Güemes and his gaucho cavalry, redirecting the revolution's strategic axis westward toward Cuyo.

In the fourth phase, San Martín assumes the Governorship of Cuyo and establishes the sprawling Campamento de El Plumerillo outside Mendoza. Unlocking San Martín as an active field operative requires fulfilling demanding logistical criteria: establishing raw material supply chains to feed Fray Luis Beltrán’s ordnance foundries, outfitting three thousand infantrymen with local textiles and leather, and concluding diplomatic pacts (parlamentos) with southern Pehuenche caciques to secure mountain passes and mislead Royalist spies. Once El Plumerillo achieves maximum military output and the Cuyo border is fortified, General José de San Martín joins the active player roster as a deployable commander possessing exceptional leadership, tactical vision, and combat bonuses.

Regional Factions and Political Alignment

Strategic diplomacy requires balancing six factions whose dynamic reputation scales dictate recruit availability, trade tariffs, local militia morale, and regional stability. Sector control directly affects these standings based on how players interact with municipal institutions and local populations.

The central government in Buenos Aires, represented by the Patriot Directory, seeks centralized administrative authority, professional military standardization, and revenue collection through customs duties. Liberating urban centers and suppressing Royalist naval blockades dramatically improves standing with the Directory, yielding financial subsidies and European arms shipments. Conversely, imposing heavy requisitions or disregarding national tax edicts provokes political friction.

In the rugged northern provinces of Salta and Jujuy, the Gauchos de Güemes—known colloquially as Los Infernales—wage asymmetrical guerrilla warfare against invading Spanish columns. Their loyalty depends on respecting regional autonomy and supporting their frontline defense. Providing carbines, lead, and horses to northern estancias elevates this relationship to allied status, unlocking elite irregular scouts; conversely, expropriating cattle or attempting to enforce rigid centralist discipline alienation turns the local population hostile.

The Regimientos de Pardos y Morenos represent the free black and freedman population whose units formed the shock infantry backbone of the revolutionary armies. Their loyalty increases when the player liberates enslaved populations from captured royalist properties, enforces the Libertad de Vientres decree, and commissions Afro-Argentine officers. A strong alignment ensures high recruitment numbers for disciplined line infantry and resilient artillery crews.

Foreign volunteers, privateers, and British merchants operate primarily across maritime corridors, the port of Buenos Aires, and riverine trade depots. Motivated by commercial access and naval commissions, their standing is tied directly to regular payments in silver, privateering captures, and the protection of merchant shipping against Spanish corsairs. Maintaining good relations unlocks access to British military-pattern flintlocks and naval ordnance.

Along the southern Andean foothills and the Pampas border, Indigenous confederacies—including Pehuenche and Ranquel tribes—control territorial corridors and access to critical mountain tracks. Their diplomatic posture is governed by formal parlamentos, reciprocal trade (yerba mate, textiles, knives), and respect for their autonomy. Betraying treaties or encroaching upon tribal grazing lands incites lightning frontier counter-raids that can disrupt logistical convoys traversing the interior.

Finally, the Spanish Royalists and absolutist partisans represent the immutable enemy faction. Sustained Royalist control over a sector imposes martial law, disarms the civilian population, and extracts resources to fund imperial counter-invasions, creating a persistent economic drain until the sector is liberated.

2. Mechanic Re-skinning and System Conversions

Recruitment Framework: Logia Lautaro and Clandestine Bulletins

The in-game digital interface of Jagged Alliance 2 is converted into a period-authentic desk view displaying handwritten broadsheets, wax-sealed letters, and leather-bound service records.

The Association of International Mercenaries (A.I.M.) is re-engineered as the clandestine roster of the Logia Lautaro. This secret revolutionary society—established historically by San Martín, Carlos María de Alvear, and Matías Zapiola—operates as a network of committed officers, veteran Creoles with European combat experience, and ideologically motivated foreign fighters. Inductions into this roster are framed as solemn revolutionary oaths. Contracts do not expire on arbitrary daily timers; instead, operatives demand monthly stipends (sueldos de campaña) disbursed via bills of exchange drawn upon the national treasury, alongside assurances that campaign objectives align with continental independence.

The secondary recruitment organization, M.E.R.C., is transformed into El Boletín Revolucionario Cívico. This administrative board is maintained by municipal Cabildos to mobilize local heroes, displaced provincial militia officers, escaped indentured peons, and rough-and-tumble gaucho fighters. Operatives hired from this bulletin demand modest compensation, arrive with rustic indigenous weapons such as shortened escopetas and work knives, and possess unrefined initial statistics that improve dramatically as they gain combat experience across the campaign.

Patriot Officer Creation: Examen de Aptitud del Cabildo

The modern psychological evaluation of the Institute for Mercenary Profiling (I.M.P.) is reimagined as an administrative and martial examination conducted by the military commission of the Cabildo de Buenos Aires. The questionnaire evaluates the candidate’s social background, equestrian training, doctrinal philosophy, and leadership temperament during crisis moments.

The v1.13 New Trait System (NTS) is re-mapped into historical competencies that reflect early 19th-century martial disciplines:

• The Cavalry Commander trait replaces Auto Weapons / Heavy Weapons proficiencies, substantially decreasing Action Point (AP) costs when mounting, maneuvering, and fighting from horseback, while conferring defensive morale bonuses to adjacent mounted troops.

• The Guerrilla Tactician trait adapts the Ranger and Stealth mechanics, eliminating movement stamina penalties when navigating broken scrub, increasing concealment within tall pampas grass, and granting ambush critical bonuses.

• The Maestro Armero / Artillerist trait adapts Technician and Bombardier skills, accelerating the field clearing and reloading of smoothbore ordnance, reducing misfire rates in adverse weather, and optimizing the repair of damaged flintlocks in camp workshops.

• The Tirador de Línea trait converts modern sniper mechanics to rifled muzzleloaders such as the British Baker rifle, improving target acquisition speeds and mitigating accuracy penalties when aiming through lingering black powder smoke.

Strategic Transport Logistics

The strategic transport functionality originally provided by the helicopter pilot Skyrider is mapped across three distinct historical transportation networks:

The Chasque Courier and Posta Relay Network governs rapid overland tactical transport. By securing post-houses (postas) along the historical Camino Real, players establish horse relays that allow small operative detachments to traverse flat provinces at high speed. Utilizing this system consumes horse stamina and requires purchasing remounts from local estancias.

The Flotilla Fluvial del Plata replaces air transport along the eastern theater. Players can commission armed river schooners, gunboats (balandras artilladas), and swift feluccas operating out of Buenos Aires, Rosario, and Santa Fe. Riverine travel bypasses impassable wetlands and muddy roads, allowing squads to rapidly redeploy heavy field pieces along the Paraná and Uruguay river corridors while executing amphibious flanking maneuvers against Royalist ports.

The Tropas de Carretas de Cuyo represent the heavy supply trains essential for sustained campaigns. These lumbering, two-wheeled ox-drawn carts transport heavy artillery, gunpowder barrels, forge equipment, and stockpiled uniforms between industrial centers and forward cantonments. While their strategic speed is low, they possess vast inventory capacities and are vital for stocking forward supply depots.

Arms Procurement: Contrabando and Domestic Maestranzas

The online firearms store Bobby Ray’s is converted into two distinct supply mechanisms that model the wartime procurement realities of the 1810s:

The Contrabando del Río de la Plata represents trade with British merchant vessels that operate covertly along the coast or anchor at shallow roadsteads off Ensenada de Barragán. Through foreign merchant factors, players purchase surplus British military hardware, including India Pattern Brown Bess muskets, Baker rifles, officers' sabres, and refined English flintstones. These transactions demand liquid silver cash, and shipments arrive with a variable delay of 72 to 120 hours via clandestine coastal couriers.

The Maestranza y Sala de Armas del Estado provides domestic industrial production. Unlocked by capturing and funding state arsenals—primarily the Retiro workshops in Buenos Aires and Fray Luis Beltrán’s foundries in Mendoza—this interface allows players to forge cavalry sabres, recondition captured Spanish muskets, cast bronze artillery from melted church bells, and produce paper powder cartridges. Production throughput scales with regional control of copper deposits, timber, and saltpeter deposits.

Territorial Economy: Estancias, Foundries, and Customs

The mineral mines of Arulco are replaced by the authentic economic engines of the Río de la Plata:

• Grandes Estancias y Saladeros generate the bulk of regional income through the cattle industry. Liberated sectors provide regular silver revenues derived from the production and sale of salted beef (tasajo), tallow, and cured hides. However, sector productivity is vulnerable to disruption; if enemy raiding parties sweep through pasturelands, livestock is scattered or destroyed, requiring several game weeks of peace to recover.

• The Aduana de Buenos Aires y Puertos Litorales represents maritime customs collection, yielding massive recurring revenues from import-export tariffs. This revenue drops dramatically whenever Royalist naval squadrons establish blockades across the river mouth, directly linking the player's financial health to riverine defense.

• Reales Cajas y Casas de Moneda represent provincial treasuries located in historical administrative centers such as Córdoba, Salta, and Mendoza. Securing these sectors yields substantial immediate cash reserves, followed by modest ongoing municipal tax revenues.

Militia Training Doctrine: Cívicos, Montoneras, and Granaderos

Training civilian militia to defend liberated territory is restructured to reflect 19th-century social mobilization:

• Milicianos Cívicos Urbanos (Green Tier): Recruited from urban townsfolk, guild apprentices, and shopkeepers. Armed with pikes, hunting escopetas, or obsolete flintlocks, these units are inexpensive to raise and garrison captured settlements effectively, though their morale is fragile under artillery bombardment.

• Montoneras Provinciales (Regular Tier): Recruited from rural gauchos, drovers, and farm hands. Equipped with lances, boleadoras, and fighting knives, they excel at skirmishing, leveraging terrain concealment, and mounting aggressive harassment strikes against enemy supply columns.

• Regimiento de Granaderos a Caballo y Batallones de Línea (Veteran Tier): Elite regulars trained under strict European tactical drill. Armed with standardized military-pattern muskets, socket bayonets, and precision-tempered sabres, they possess high morale stability, form defensive squares against cavalry, and execute devastating bayonet charges without breaking formation.

3. Tactical Combat and Black Powder Ballistics

Firearm Dynamics in the 100-AP Framework

Adapting the Jagged Alliance 2 v1.13 tactical combat engine to early 19th-century black powder warfare requires sweeping changes to weapon balance, line-of-sight algorithms, and the underlying Action Point (AP) economy. Within the expanded 100-AP framework, smoothbore and rifled muzzleloaders are rebalanced around prolonged reload cycles offset by lethal kinetic trauma.

Reloading an avancarga smoothbore musket—such as the Brown Bess or Charleville Modèle 1777—requires biting the paper cartridge, pouring gunpowder down the muzzle, seating the spherical lead ball with a ramrod, priming the flashpan, and cocking the hammer. In the modified engine, this multi-step process demands 40 to 50 AP. Rifled muskets utilizing tightly patched lead balls, such as the Baker rifle, demand 65 to 75 AP to cycle completely. Furthermore, these reloading animations are strictly restricted by stance flags: reloading can only be executed while standing or kneeling. Attempting to reload while prone is disabled, or incurs a severe 50% AP penalty, forcing soldiers to expose themselves during reloading cycles.

Ballistic flight modeling in Weapons.xml reflects the realities of spherical soft-lead projectiles fired through unrifled barrels. Smoothbore weapons exhibit sharp ballistic dispersion beyond point-blank distances. Chance to Hit (CTH) is calibrated to 85% at close quarters (0 to 15 tiles), dropping sharply to 35% at medium range (25 tiles), and decaying to less than 10% beyond 35 tiles. Conversely, rifled arms maintain tight ballistic cones out to 50 tiles, providing unmatched long-range precision at the cost of slow cycling times.

Every discharge of a black powder weapon spawns an expanding, opaque smoke cloud object directly on the firer's tile that lingers across multiple turns. These volumetric clouds obstruct line of sight, degrade long-range accuracy by 40% to 70%, and break tactical interrupts. Following two or three coordinated volleys, battlefields become shrouded in blinding white smoke, obscuring visibility and setting the stage for decisive close-quarters melee assaults.

The reliability calculation in the combat engine is updated to model flintlock ignition failure influenced by weather and fouling:

$$\text{Chance of Misfire} = \text{Base Inaccuracy} + (100 - \text{Item Condition}) \times 0.20 + \text{Precipitation Intensity} \times 0.50 + \text{Relative Humidity Modifier}$$

During heavy pampas downpours or morning river fogs, misfire rates escalate substantially. A failed roll triggers a "Flash in the Pan" event: the powder in the external priming pan burns off without igniting the main powder charge within the breech, expending the trigger AP and requiring a 15-AP remedial action to re-prime the lock. Furthermore, striking flints lose durability with every discharge, requiring operatives to keep replacement flints in their personal load-bearing equipment.

Melee Combat Mechanics and Morale Shock

Because firearms are slow to reload and inaccurate over distance, cold steel serves as the decisive battlefield weapon. The combat engine is revised to prioritize melee charges, weapon reach, and morale shock:

The Charge Action allows an operative to expend their entire remaining AP pool in a straight-line sprint toward an enemy, culminating in a devastating thrust or slash. For every tile traversed in a continuous charge sprint, the final attack gains a cumulative +10% kinetic damage multiplier and increased armor penetration, reflecting the physical momentum of the runner or charging horse.

Weapon types fulfill distinct tactical roles:

• The Sable Corvo (curved sabre) features low swing costs (12 to 14 AP) and deals massive slashing damage that inflicts immediate, heavy bleeding wounds.

• The Bayoneta de Cubo (socket bayonet), affixed to the muzzle of an unloaded musket, transforms the firearm into an eight-foot spear, granting a two-tile tactical reach, defensive intercept bonuses against enemy melee charges, and piercing damage capable of puncturing heavy uniforms.

• The Lanza de Tacuara (gaucho lance) delivers extreme shock damage when wielded by mounted troops, knocking enemy infantry off their feet or out of the saddle.

• The Facón Gaucho (heavy utility knife) allows swift, close-range defensive parries (8 AP) when combined with an off-hand wrapped poncho (poncho al brazo), which absorbs incoming blade strikes.

The suppression mechanic is overhauled into a Morale Shock Engine. When an infantry formation is targeted by an organized bayonet advance or a mounted cavalry charge, every soldier in the impact zone must pass a morale check scaled against the incoming unit's Leadership and raw combat speed. Conscripts or low-morale militia who fail this check break formation immediately, drop their weapons, and flee toward the sector boundary, reproducing historical routs observed during the revolutionary wars.

Artillery and Crew-Served Ordnance

Crew-served artillery replaces modern mortar and missile slots in the engine:

Smoothbore bronze field cannons (4-pounder and 8-pounder pieces) operate as multi-crew tactical installations requiring a gunner to establish line-of-sight elevation and a loader to swab, ram, and prime the tube. Repositioning and pivoting these field pieces consumes significant AP, encouraging players to site guns defensively along likely avenues of approach.

Solid iron round shot delivers flat-trajectory direct fire that obliterates stone barricades, punches through adobe walls, and travels through multiple human targets caught in its line of flight, inflicting immediate lethal damage. Conversely, canister and grapeshot transform artillery into devastating close-range weapons. Upon discharge, the canister ruptures at the muzzle, projecting a widening spread of iron balls across a 25-tile cone. Grapeshot inflicts massive suppression, limb loss, and casualty spikes, devastating tight infantry ranks caught in the open.

Smaller ordnance, including pedreros (swivel guns) and lightweight mountain howitzers, can be packed onto pack mules or mounted along the gunwales of armed river schooners, offering versatile fire support in rugged terrain and naval boardings.

4. Campaign Map and Sector Architecture

Operational Sectors of the Argentine Campaign Map

The strategic map comprises four operational theaters, tracking the historical geography of the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata:

The Buenos Aires and Riverine Litoral theater encompasses the political capital, primary customs houses, and fortified naval strongholds along the river system. Controlling Buenos Aires (Sectors G8, G9, H8) provides the financial and administrative core of the revolution, housing the central Cabildo and the military workshops at Retiro. Northward along the Paraná River lie Santa Fe, San Nicolás, and Rosario (Sectors E7, F7, F8), which are vulnerable to amphibious Royalist landings and corsair blockades originating from the estuary.

The Central Crossroads of Córdoba (Sectors F4, F5) forms the strategic hub linking the capital to the interior provinces. Córdoba is essential for inland logistical routing and houses the historic Jesuit Estancia of Caroya, the primary domestic workshop for manufacturing sabres, lances, and leather accoutrements.

The Cuyo and Andean Frontier (Sectors G1, H1, H2, H3) comprises the provinces of Mendoza and San Juan, backed against the Andes mountains. Mendoza hosts the Campamento de El Plumerillo, where San Martín and Fray Luis Beltrán organize logistics and manufacture artillery. Farther west lie the rugged mountain passes of Uspallata and Los Patos, acting as formidable defensive barriers against Royalist incursions.

The Northern War of Attrition (Sectors A1, A2, B2, D4) spans the frontline territories of Tucumán, Salta, and Jujuy. Tucumán acts as a central fortified redoubt, anchored by the Ciudadela fortress. Salta and Jujuy represent the active operational zone where Güemes’s gauchos wage continuous partisan warfare. At the northern limit lies the Quebrada de Humahuaca (Sector A1), a deep mountain gorge that serves as the entry bottleneck for invading Royalist armies descending from Upper Peru.

Strategic Choke Points and Supply Routes

Strategic logistics depend entirely on maintaining control over the primary trade arteries:

• The Camino Real del Norte connects Buenos Aires through Córdoba and Tucumán up to the Quebrada de Humahuaca. Transporting munitions, lead, and troop reinforcements along this road is necessary to sustain the Northern Army. If Royalist forces or loyalist partisans capture intermediate post-houses, such as Santiago del Estero, forward garrisons in Salta and Jujuy suffer supply cutoffs, causing their combat performance and recovery rates to deteriorate.

• The Paso de San Nicolás on the Paraná River acts as an amphibious choke point. Enemy dominance along this river corridor severs the connection between the coastal capital and the western interior, preventing waterborne supply and forcing detachments onto difficult overland routes.

• The High Andean Passes (Uspallata and Los Patos) serve as rugged natural bottlenecks where small, well-placed detachments can repel substantially larger advancing forces.

Environmental Hazards and Biome Modifiers

Combat maps feature distinct environmental parameters based on local geography:

The Litoral Deltas and Swamps feature deep mud, thick marsh grass, and torrential cloudbursts. Slogging through deep mud increases movement AP costs by 50% for infantry and 100% for mounted cavalry. High humidity rapidly rusts metal components, doubling the rate of firearm fouling and increasing flintlock misfire rates during rainstorms.

The Pampas and Monte Scrublands present flat terrain covered with tall wild grasses and scattered scrub. Operatives face intense summer heat, which rapidly drains stamina and accelerates dehydration during extended maneuvers. The open terrain offers little natural vertical cover, requiring soldiers to utilize tall brush for concealment, lay prone, or execute swift cavalry strikes.

The High Andean Passes and Quebradas feature thin atmosphere, steep vertical drops, and sub-zero night temperatures. Operatives without cold-weather wool ponchos suffer ongoing stamina damage and hypothermia. Unacclimated soldiers suffer from apunamiento (altitude sickness), which reduces their maximum AP pool by 20% to 30% unless offset by high physical Health and Strength. Winter snows completely close these alpine sectors, preventing all transit.

Royalist Strategic AI Logic

The Spanish Strategic AI pursues clear operational objectives rather than dispatching random patrols:

• When Patriot control over the northern provinces falters, the AI organizes an expeditionary corps in Upper Peru—combining Peninsular line infantry, regular cavalry, and field artillery—and drives south down the Humahuaca canyon to retake Jujuy, Salta, and Tucumán.

• Along the coast, the Royalist naval AI tracks customs revenues in Buenos Aires and Santa Fe. If Patriot maritime income rises above target thresholds, the AI launches amphibious flotillas to raid coastal towns, bombard shore batteries, and sever supply routes.

• In interior provinces with low popular loyalty, the AI equips Loyalist partisans to conduct guerrilla raids against Patriot estancias, burning crops and slaughtering cattle to bleed the revolutionary treasury.

5. Historical Operative Roster

Operative Roster and Statistical Profiles

The operative roster features historical figures and period-accurate soldiers. Attributes and skills operate on the standard 0–100 scale: Health (HEA), Agility (AGI), Dexterity (DEX), Strength (STR), Leadership (LEA), Wisdom (WIS), Marksmanship (MRK), Mechanical (MEC), Explosives (EXP), and Medical (MED). Combat specialties are assigned using the v1.13 New Trait System.

Martín Miguel de Güemes

• Role: Guerrilla Warlord & Vanguard Cavalry Commander.

7. Technical Implementation and Engine Conversion

Adapting the Jagged Alliance 2 v1.13 C++ codebase into GRANADEROS transitions an established tactical engine into a historical simulation of the Argentine War of Independence. By utilizing the modularity of the v1.13 Tabledata XML directory structure—particularly Weapons.xml, Items.xml, MercProfiles.xml, and Cities.xml—the historical conversion is achieved while preserving the underlying tactical depth.

At the strategic level, the modern mercenary economic cycle is successfully adapted to 19th-century reality: clandestine revolutionary registries replace corporate merc networks, riverine gunboats and horse relays replace helicopters, and livestock estancias replace mineral extraction. These strategic conversions directly feed into the core campaign progression, where player investments into logistics, ordnance foundries, and regional alliances pave the way for unlocking General José de San Martín and launching the final liberation offensives.

Tactically, black powder ballistics, smoke obscuration, weather-induced ignition failure, and devastating melee charges transform the pacing of turn-based combat. Firefights become deliberate engagements defined by slow reload cycles and smoke-covered corridors, where victory is decided by combined-arms coordination: pinning opposing formations with musket volleys, unleashing canister shot from field guns, and executing decisive saber charges with elite Granaderos. This design framework delivers an authentic tactical simulation that honors the military history of the South American Wars of Independence.
